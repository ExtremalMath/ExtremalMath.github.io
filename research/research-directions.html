Research Directions

Short, textbook-grounded introductions to the areas I am currently studying.

The three directions below are the focus of my current reading and study. Each summary is drawn from standard graduate texts, with references listed at the end of every section.

Extremal Graph Theory

Extremal graph theory asks how large a graph — measured by edges, vertices, or another parameter — can become before a prescribed property is forced to appear. Its central object is the extremal function ex(n; H), the maximum number of edges in an n-vertex graph containing no copy of a fixed graph H. Turán's theorem gives the exact value when H is a complete graph, and the Erdős–Stone theorem determines the asymptotic answer for every non-bipartite H; it is often called the fundamental theorem of the field. For bipartite forbidden graphs much remains open, the Zarankiewicz problem being a famous example. The subject also studies structure rather than size: Szemerédi's regularity lemma approximates every large graph by a bounded number of pseudorandom pairs of parts, and the graph removal lemma shows that near-copies of H can be made exact by deleting few edges. Current developments include the probabilistic method, dependent random choice, hypergraph regularity, and graph limits, in which dense graph sequences converge to limit objects called graphons. My own reading centers on H-free and Turán-type problems for multipartite graphs.

References: B. Bollobás, Extremal Graph Theory (Dover, 2004) and Modern Graph Theory (Springer, 1998); J. A. Bondy and U. S. R. Murty, Graph Theory (Springer, 2008); L. Lovász, Large Networks and Graph Limits (AMS, 2012).

Functional Analysis

Functional analysis is the study of infinite-dimensional vector spaces — Banach and Hilbert spaces — and of the linear operators acting on them; it supplies the common language of modern analysis. Three results form its backbone: the Hahn–Banach theorem, which extends linear functionals without increasing their norm; the Baire category consequences — uniform boundedness, the open mapping, and the closed graph theorems; and duality, through which a space is understood by its continuous linear functionals. Weak and weak-* topologies make this duality flexible, while the Banach–Alaoglu theorem provides compactness of the dual unit ball, and reflexivity brings that compactness back to the space itself. Compact operators then lead to the Fredholm alternative and, for self-adjoint operators on Hilbert spaces, to the spectral theorem. For partial differential equations these tools are decisive: they convert questions about unknown functions into questions about operators between function spaces, where existence and uniqueness can be attacked systematically. I am currently reading Brezis with emphasis on the analytic machinery used for elliptic and evolution equations.

References: H. Brezis, Functional Analysis, Sobolev Spaces and Partial Differential Equations (Springer, 2011); W. Rudin, Functional Analysis (McGraw-Hill, 2nd ed., 1991); J. B. Conway, A Course in Functional Analysis (Springer).

Partial Differential Equations

A partial differential equation (PDE) is an equation relating an unknown function of several variables to its partial derivatives. PDEs model most continuum phenomena in nature — heat conduction, wave propagation, electrostatics, fluid flow — and the theory organizes second-order linear equations into three classes: elliptic, such as the Laplace and Poisson equations; parabolic, such as the heat equation; and hyperbolic, such as the wave equation. The classical program, going back to Hadamard, asks whether a problem is well posed: existence, uniqueness, and continuous dependence on data. Because classical smooth solutions often do not exist, the modern theory is built on weak solutions, formulated in Sobolev spaces through integration by parts. Weak formulations turn boundary-value problems into variational problems: the Lax–Milgram lemma gives existence and uniqueness for coercive elliptic problems, energy methods supply a priori estimates, and regularity theory recovers smoothness, with the maximum principle providing further structure. I am currently studying weak solutions of elliptic and evolution equations, with regularity questions as my main focus.

References: L. C. Evans, Partial Differential Equations (AMS, 2nd ed., 2010); H. Brezis, Functional Analysis, Sobolev Spaces and Partial Differential Equations (Springer, 2011); D. Gilbarg and N. S. Trudinger, Elliptic Partial Differential Equations of Second Order (Springer, 1983).
